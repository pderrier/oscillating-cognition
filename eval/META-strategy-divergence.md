# Why Two Judges Disagreed on Strategy — And What It Means

**Context:** Two AI judges (GPT-4o and Claude Opus) evaluated the same pair of strategic option sets for an AI-tutor pivot. GPT preferred Output B. Opus preferred Output A. Same criteria, opposite verdicts.

---

## The Outputs

**Output A** — three options that treat the AI tutor as pedagogical provocation:
1. "Graduate the Tutor" — make itself progressively unnecessary
2. "Friction by Design" — sometimes resist and offer challengeable guidance
3. "Memory With Blind Spots" — deliberately forget, force re-derivation

**Output B** — three options that span a risk spectrum:
1. "Own The Practice Loop" — narrow domain, measure lift
2. "Canonical Tutor Hybrid" — AI + vetted human content
3. "Kill The Course Catalog" — outcome tracks, not courses

---

## Analysis

### 1. What "good strategy" means is ambiguous at its core

The disagreement exposes a fault line between *generative insight* and *decision utility*. A reframes the problem ("what if the tutor's job is to destabilize the learner?"). B solves the problem ("what's our Monday morning move?"). Both are legitimate strategic work, but they serve different moments in a decision process. A is pre-strategic (expanding the option space), B is strategic in the operational sense (narrowing toward commitment). The judges disagree because the task — "strategic options" — is ambiguous about which moment it serves.

### 2. Same criteria, different implicit weighting

Both judges use the same five criteria. But GPT weights actionability and decision-readiness as near-prerequisites — cleverness without executability is discounted. Opus weights non-obviousness and tradeoff clarity as intellectually prior — originality earns credit even when executability is weak.

Opus also evaluates set-level properties: A's options cluster in one quadrant (all pedagogically contrarian), while B's span a risk spectrum. GPT evaluates options more individually. A set of three brilliant but redundant options is less useful than three mediocre but complementary ones — whether you notice depends on whether you treat the output as a portfolio or a list.

### 3. The verdicts are reconcilable — by splitting the question

Opus nearly does this itself: *"If the ask were 'which set do I hand to an exec team on Monday,' the answer flips to Y."* The two judges answer different implicit questions:
- GPT: which output is more **useful to a decision-maker**?
- Opus: which output demonstrates stronger **strategic thinking**?

No single ranking can serve both without specifying the use-case.

### 4. What this reveals about AI judges

1. **AI judges are sensitive to implicit weighting in ways that are hard to audit.** Same rubric, opposite conclusions — the rubric underdetermines the verdict.
2. **Granular scores produce falsifiable evaluations.** Opus's per-criterion scores let you argue with specific claims. GPT's preference-only output is opaque.
3. **Two judges reveal what one judge hides.** The disagreement itself is the most informative signal. A single-judge system would have presented one answer as authoritative. The internal tension between originality and executability would have stayed invisible.

---

## Implication for the Project

This suggests strategy mode needs **two sub-modes** or an explicit framing parameter:
- **Explore** — "expand my option space" → optimize for non-obviousness
- **Decide** — "help me pick Monday morning" → optimize for actionability and portfolio coverage

The same upstream thinking (oscillation or baseline) may need different grounding depending on where the user is in their decision process.
